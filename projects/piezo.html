Energy-harvesting Computer Keys — Research & Prototypes

Revolutionizing typing: harvesting mechanical energy from keystrokes

Independent research project • multi-year effort • ISEF presenter • provisional patent

In a science research course I chose to explore energy harvesting because I wanted to work on a practical climate-related problem. What began as a class exercise and a slide-deck grew into a four-year independent research journey: I read dozens of papers, reached out to authors by email and Zoom for feedback, learned physics and 3D printing on my own, sourced components, built test rigs, and iterated prototypes. When internships weren’t available coming out of COVID, I taught myself the tools and techniques needed to test feasibility.

That work produced a reproducible computer-key design integrating piezoelectric material, multiple rigorous tests (force profiling with a Vernier force probe and an SG90 motor-driven repeatable keystroke), presentations at Regeneron ISEF (2023) and a provisional patent — and ultimately a prototype key that produced measurable voltage from single keystrokes. Summary — methods & results

Condensed from the project paper (technical details and full datasets in the PDF). :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

  • Design approach: 3D-printed key housing integrating a piezoelectric plate in the switch base (printed stems/modified housing to press piezo plate).
  • Testing rig: measured average keystroke force with a Vernier force probe (average peak ≈ 1.667 N) and used an SG90-driven actuator to apply repeatable force. (See equipment/setup in paper.)
  • Material selection: compared multiple APC PZT elements; chose Item 1738 for a balance of voltage and thickness after comparative testing. (Charts in the paper show material comparisons.)
  • Electronics: piezo wiring → bridge rectifier → measurement via Vernier voltage probe and data logging; experiments include chained piezo arrays and single-key tests.
  • Maximum measured voltage per keystroke (key prototype): 0.403 V (test using ~0.6 N in key assembly). :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}
  • Chained piezo elements produced higher open-circuit voltages individually but lost voltage once soldered in chaining (assembly effects noted).
  • Conclusion: feasible proof-of-concept for harvesting typing energy; not yet sufficient to fully self-charge a laptop but promising for incremental energy capture and future material improvements.
  • Awards & IP: ISEF presentation(s) and provisional patent issued during this research. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}
