Energy-Harvesting Computer Keys — Research & Prototype

Project story

4 years of independent research · ISEF finalist · Provisional patent awarded

In a science course exercise I first sketched the idea that typing on laptops might be a small, widely-available source of mechanical energy. After COVID disrupted opportunities for internships, I taught myself the tools — reading ~100 papers, emailing and Zooming with researchers, learning 3D modelling and printing, and ordering/assembling materials. What began as a classroom exercise turned into a multi-year independent research project: building prototypes, designing tests, presenting at ISEF (2023), earning 4th place in Energy: Sustainable Materials & Design and a provisional patent — and iterating toward a single reproducible energy-harvesting computer key that integrates piezoelectric material into a mechanical switch.

This page summarizes the project’s core sections (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Limitations & Conclusion) and includes photos, video, and a downloadable copy of the full paper.

Paper summary by section

Introduction

The paper motivates using piezoelectric energy harvesting to reduce everyday device dependence on non-renewable electricity. It identifies laptops as a high-impact target (many devices, frequent charging) and frames keystroke mechanical energy as an overlooked, distributed source worth exploring. The study positions a practical proof-of-concept for a self-charging computer via piezoelectric transducers. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

Methods & Key Design Decisions

The project ran in two phases. Phase I tested chained piezo disks under a laptop keyboard to capture typing vibrations. Phase II focused on a reproducible single mechanical key: a reworked switch housing, shortened stem, and a piezoelectric plate positioned beneath the stem. Tests used a Vernier force probe and an SG90 servo to apply repeatable keystroke forces; multiple PZT materials were measured for voltage response. Prototype electronics included a bridge rectifier and measurement via Vernier interfaces.

Results

Measurements showed positive voltage generation from piezo elements under keystroke forces. Individual piezos produced peaks from ~0.8–2.4 V in bench tests, but chaining and soldering reduced single-element output (statistically significant drop). The engineered single key (with Item 1738 PZT) produced up to ~0.403 V per keystroke under controlled force. Typing speed had less impact than applied pressure.

Discussion

The data support feasibility at proof-of-concept scale: keystrokes can generate measurable voltage, and careful material & geometry choices matter. The study finds that thinner piezo plates performed better for the application, and that assembly methods (soldering/chaining) and material brittleness limit practical output. The project also highlights how reproducible single-key designs enable clearer comparisons across materials.

Limitations, Future Work & Conclusion

Major limitations were access to a wider range of modern flexible piezoelectric materials, brittleness of PZT ceramics, assembly variability, and the aged test laptop. Future work recommends testing newer thin/flexible transducers, optimizing electrode geometry, and improving integration methods. The paper concludes the work is a meaningful step toward self-charging computing, while noting the gap between proof-of-concept output and a fully charging laptop.
