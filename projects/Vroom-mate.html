Vroom-mate — quick remote dorm vacuum (1-week build)

Project overview

Course: ME490 Product Design · Role: Designer & builder · Tools: SolidWorks, Illustrator, Arduino, laser cutter, 3D printers · Week: 1

In the first week of ME490 the brief was intentionally broad: build a solution to a problem using available campus resources. Vroom-mate is a compact, remote-control vacuum for dorm rooms — a pared-down Roomba that you can drive with an IR remote, built from lab scavenged parts in a week.

Key Components
  • Design inspiration & brief Campus kit + limited time: a simple remote vacuum to reduce manual crawling with a handheld vacuum in dorms.
  • Mechanical base & shell Laser-cut wooden base, 3-wheel drivetrain, 3D-printed lid and dust cylinder — sized to fit under dorm furniture and house components.
  • Electronics & control Arduino microcontroller + IR receiver (remote control for forward/back/left/right and fan on/off) and motor driver for two drive motors.
  • Collection & airflow A fan draws light debris into a 3D-printed cylinder lined with breathable fabric to trap particles while allowing airflow.
  • Power Separate battery supplies: 9V for fan, 4×AA for Arduino & motors, mounted for easy replacement.
  • Rapid prototyping approach Solderless breadboard testing, laser cutting, and multi-part 3D printing (split prints to fit printers) for a one-week turnaround.

Play-by-play: week-long build

Each step can have 1–6 photos — click a card to view the step gallery.
1 — Problem discovery
Noticed vacuuming in dorm was tedious; Roomba would solve it but a full robot is overkill — aim for a handheld remote vacuum base.
2 — Concept & sizing
Designed a 1-ft diameter base with a 6″ fan hole; sketched slots for a 3-wheel layout in Illustrator for laser cutting.
3 — Fabrication: base
Cut wood base on the laser, mounted motors, axel (nail + washers) for the free wheel, and secured components.
4 — Electronics & IR
Wired Arduino, IR receiver, motor driver, fan, and buttons on a solderless breadboard. Programmed forward/back/left/right + fan toggle.
5 — 3D printing & fit
Designed top in SolidWorks and printed in four parts (to fit printers). Printed dust cylinder and rim to secure fabric filter.
6 — Test & finish
Tested pickup on pom-poms & yarn, adjusted battery arrangement (9V fan, 4×AA motors), glued final assembly, painted the lid black.
