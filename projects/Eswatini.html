Nyakuza Foot Bridge — Eswatini, Africa

I spent eight weeks in Eswatini working alongside a team of Duke classmates, Engineers In Action staff, local masons, and community members to design and construct the Nyakuza Suspended Footbridge—the longest pedestrian bridge in the country at 128 meters.

Prior to traveling in-country, I assisted with the bridge’s design during the academic year, participating in engineering review calls and collaborating on structural decisions. While on-site, I worked directly with community members to ensure construction quality and safety.

Project snapshot

On-site work

Construction involved local masonry, tensioning of suspension cables, and careful anchoring to bedrock and retaining structures. We adapted standard suspension bridge techniques to the local context and materials availability.

Community partnership

Coordination with local leaders and masons ensured the bridge fit community needs, created local employment, and built skills for maintenance and future projects.

Quality control

My role focused on checking dimensions, verifying splice quality, and confirming safety margins for foot traffic. I documented construction progress and non-conformances for follow-up.

Project details

Design & Analysis
Structural design, load calculations, and material selection.

The bridge was sized for pedestrian loads with conservative safety factors to account for dynamic loading and uneven distributions. Design included calculations for cable tension, deck loads, and anchor block sizing.

  • Capacity sizing for 2–3 people per linear meter
  • Anchor block design using locally-sourced stone and concrete
  • Simplified analysis for maintainability by local masons
Construction sequence
How the bridge was built step-by-step on site.

Construction began with site clearing, footing excavation, and anchor placement. Cable installation was staged with temporary supports and tensioned progressively as the deck was laid.

  • Local masonry for anchor and approach ramps
  • Staged cable tensioning to control deflection
  • Final decking and handrail installation with community labor
Materials & tools
What we used and why — cables, anchors, timber, concrete.

We balanced robustness with transportability: high-tensile steel cables, locally mixed concrete for anchor blocks, and timber decking where appropriate.

  • High-tensile galvanised suspension cable
  • Concrete mixes made with local aggregate
  • Basic lifting and tensioning tools
Site logistics
Transport, staging, and working with local constraints.

Limited road access required modular planning: materials were staged at roadheads and carried to site. Weather windows were important for tensioning operations and concrete curing.

  • Material staging plans to minimize on-site footprint
  • Coordination of local labor schedules and transport
Quality Control
Checks, tolerances, and inspection procedures.

QC covered alignment, tension checks, splice integrity, and deck fastening. Non-conformances were documented and corrected in close collaboration with field leads.

  • Measurement checks for sag and clearance
  • Splice and clamp inspection protocol
  • Documentation of corrective actions
Community & training
Skills transfer, maintenance plan, and local ownership.

We trained local teams on basic inspection and maintenance, provided documentation, and worked with local leaders to build ownership and a maintenance schedule to keep the bridge safe long-term.

  • Hands-on training for caretakers
  • Maintenance checklist and inspection cadence
  • Community handover and sustainability planning
