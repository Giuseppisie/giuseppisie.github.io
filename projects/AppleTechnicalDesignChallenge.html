Apple Technical Design Challenge Engineering analysis · Product design · Power systems

Challenge Overview

Technical design application · Five-question response and supporting documentation

This page documents my response to Apple's Technical Design Challenge, beginning with an everyday powered object and tracing the engineering systems that enable its use. I chose my laptop, focusing on web browsing and research as a daily use case.

The investigation builds on my earlier work studying piezoelectric energy harvesting in computer keyboards, connecting an interest in sustainable electronics with mechanical engineering and user-centered design.

Challenge Questions

Question 1 — Everyday Object & Power Path

Question 1

Describe why you chose the object in your house used every day that requires power. Describe the main use case for the object. How you interact with the object. Describe the power path for this object to enable your daily use case.

My Response

I have been interested in the intersection of user-facing product design and clean energy since sophomore year of high school, when I spent two years researching energy harvesting technologies in consumer electronics. My project focused on designing a piezoelectric computer key capable of converting typing forces into electricity. Although the prototype demonstrated that a potential difference can be generated by combining piezoelectric ceramics and into a computer key, I also became aware that this is not the most efficient solution due to the incredibly low current of the ceramics. When I saw this assignment, I knew it was an opportunity to revisit those research questions while applying the mechanical engineering knowledge I have gained over the past two years to rethink how computers could become more energy efficient.

I use a computer every day, as do billions of people around the world. Whether I am searching for a new recipe, sending emails, completing coursework, or reading research papers, my computer serves as my primary connection to information. While industries such as transportation, lighting, and household appliances have made highly visible advances toward sustainability, consumer electronics have seen comparatively little attention despite being used constantly across the globe. This made the computer an ideal object for my design exploration.

The primary use case I will analyze is web browsing and online research. During a typical session, I repeatedly type search queries, read articles, and continue refining my searches. My interaction with the computer begins with the keyboard, where each keystroke sends an electrical signal to the processor. A Wi-Fi connection transmits my request across the internet, while the processor performs millions of calculations to retrieve and organize the requested information. Finally, the graphics processor and display controller generate the image that appears on the screen, allowing me to read the results before repeating the process again.

The power path that enables this seemingly simple interaction is surprisingly complex. Electrical energy enters the computer from a wall outlet as alternating current (AC). Inside the charger, it is converted into direct current (DC) before charging the battery and supplying the system. The battery management system and power management integrated circuit regulate and distribute electricity to the processor, graphics processor, memory, storage drive, wireless networking hardware, display, keyboard, and other supporting electronics. Each component performs a specific function, but nearly all of the electrical energy entering the computer ultimately becomes heat. As I continue typing and searching, I can often feel the device warming beneath my hands; a reminder that the processor is working continuously to execute billions of operations. Understanding this complete energy pathway provides the foundation for investigating how mechanical design can reduce energy consumption rather than simply managing the heat produced afterward.

Question 2 — Average Power Measurement

Question 2

Compute the average power, in units of watts, the object requires to enable this main use case. Clearly demonstrate your work, starting from first principles. List the assumptions, theory, equations and/or method used to compute an average value in watts.

My Response

The objective of this analysis was to determine the average electrical power required for my Microsoft Surface Laptop Studio 2 to perform its primary use case: web browsing and online research. Rather than using the laptop charger’s maximum power rating, I experimentally measured energy consumption during a representative browsing session. I first calculated average power from the change in stored battery energy and then used HWiNFO64 to independently record instantaneous battery discharge power.

Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or consumed. Therefore, average power can be calculated from first principles as

Pavg=ΔE/Δt

where Pavg is average power in watts (W), ΔE is energy consumed in watt-hours (Wh), and Δt is elapsed time in hours (h). Because the battery stores energy in watt-hours, dividing the decrease in stored battery energy by time directly produces average power in watts. This method measures the total system power required by the laptop, including the processor, memory, storage, display, Wi-Fi hardware, motherboard, keyboard, and cooling system.

To approximate normal web browsing while controlling major variables, the laptop began at 100% charge and remained unplugged throughout the experiment. Screen brightness was fixed at 50%, Wi-Fi was enabled, Bluetooth was disabled, Battery Saver was disabled, and no external monitors or peripherals were connected. Google Chrome was used to conduct searches, navigate webpages, read articles, and take notes. Gaming, video streaming, software installations, and other intentionally computationally intensive activities were avoided. I assumed that the reported battery state of charge was proportional to the battery’s available stored energy and that background system activity represented normal laptop operation.

Using Windows’ powercfg/batteryreport command, I determined that the battery had a full-charge capacity of approximately 56.85 Wh. During a 30-minute browsing experiment, the displayed battery charge decreased by 13 percentage points, from 100% to 87%. The estimated electrical energy consumed was therefore

ΔE=(0.13)(56.85 Wh)=7.39 Wh

The elapsed time was Δt=0.5 hours

Therefore, Pavg=(0.50h)(7.39 Wh)=14.78 W

This first-principles calculation estimates that the laptop required an average of approximately 14.8 W during the defined browsing workload.

To independently evaluate this estimate, I performed a second test using HWiNFO64, which continuously logged the battery charge/discharge rate rather than relying on only the initial and final displayed battery percentages. The recorded power fluctuated throughout the session as webpages loaded, processor activity changed, and periods of reading required less computation. The logged dataset produced an overall average discharge magnitude of approximately 10.17 W. During this test, the laptop also shut off and turned back on due to brief perceived inactivity; this interruption is visible in the power-versus-time data and could contribute to the lower average result.

The HWiNFO measurement is lower than the 14.78 W estimate from the battery-percentage method. This difference highlights an important limitation of the first method: Windows displays battery percentage in discrete increments, so measuring a relatively small change over only 30 minutes introduces substantial uncertainty. The browsing workload also varies naturally with user activity and background processes. Continuous power logging therefore provides a higher-resolution characterization of instantaneous system behavior, while the battery-energy calculation demonstrates the average-power calculation directly from first principles.

Together, the experiments indicate that web browsing on this laptop requires power on the order of 10–15 W, with 14.78 W as the first-principles estimate. These measurements establish the baseline against which the proposed redesign can be evaluated under an identical workload.

Question 3 — Brainstorming Three Redesigns

Question 3

Describe how to redesign the object to enable your main use case to be more efficient. Identify a performance target. What does it mean to be more efficient? For example: more efficient means my main use case will be _____ compared to the original design. Brainstorm! Show pros and cons for at least 3 different ways to redesign the object to meet this new target. Clearly show your work!

My Response

To improve the energy efficiency of my laptop during web browsing, I defined a clear performance target: complete the same research task while consuming less electrical power from the battery. In other words, a more efficient laptop performs the same computations, displays the same information, and provides the same user experience while requiring fewer watts to accomplish the task. Since the primary use case is internet browsing and online research rather than computationally intensive tasks such as gaming or video rendering, the redesign should improve energy efficiency without compromising portability, user comfort, or everyday usability.

My first concept revisited the research that originally inspired me to pursue engineering: piezoelectric computer keys. Every time a user types, mechanical energy is applied to the keyboard and immediately dissipated into the surrounding structure. My original idea was to embed piezoelectric ceramics beneath into a newly designed computer key to harvest this otherwise unused mechanical energy and convert it into electricity that could supplement the battery. The primary advantage of this concept is that the energy source already exists and is completely renewable. No additional user effort is required, and every keystroke contributes a small amount of electrical energy that would otherwise be lost. Unlike solar panels or external charging systems, the energy is generated directly from normal interaction with the device.

However, after two years of research and prototyping, I concluded that the power output of commercially available piezoelectric materials is far too small to significantly offset a laptop's energy consumption. Typical web browsing requires approximately 8–10 W of continuous power, whereas individual keystrokes produce only milliwatts of power for fractions of a second. Producing meaningful energy would require increasing the force required to press each key, increasing the size of the piezoelectric elements, or significantly altering the keyboard geometry. All three solutions negatively affect typing comfort, increase manufacturing complexity, and add weight to the device. Although this concept successfully demonstrated proof of concept, it ultimately became clear that harvesting user-generated mechanical energy could not provide meaningful improvements in overall system efficiency.

This naturally led to my second concept: rather than harvesting mechanical energy, could I recover the large amount of thermal energy continuously leaving the laptop? Every watt consumed by the processor ultimately becomes heat, so I investigated thermoelectric generators (TEGs) that convert temperature differences into electricity through the Seebeck effect. In theory, waste heat could be partially recovered and redirected back into the battery or power management system. The primary advantage of this concept is that it attempts to utilize energy already being generated during normal operation instead of allowing it to dissipate unused into the surrounding environment.

After reviewing recent literature, however, several limitations became apparent. Thermoelectric generators exhibit relatively poor conversion efficiencies at the modest temperature differences typically found inside consumer electronics. More importantly, the thermoelectric module itself introduces additional thermal resistance between the processor and the cooling system. This makes it more difficult for heat to escape, increasing processor temperatures and potentially causing earlier thermal throttling. In other words, recovering a very small amount of electrical energy may actually reduce the overall efficiency of the laptop by trapping significantly more heat inside the system. Although thermoelectric generators continue to show promise in high-temperature industrial applications and automotive waste heat recovery, current material limitations make them poorly suited for thin consumer laptops.

This realization fundamentally changed my approach to the problem. Instead of trying to recover wasted energy, I began asking how the laptop could be redesigned so that less energy is wasted in the first place. Since nearly all electrical energy consumed by the processor eventually becomes heat, reducing processor temperature can improve sustained performance while decreasing cooling requirements. This observation led to my final concept: redesigning the laptop chassis into a multifunctional thermal management component.

Current laptops already use heat pipes and vapor chambers to transport heat away from the processor toward a relatively small finned heat sink where a fan removes the heat through forced convection. The aluminum chassis participates only minimally in this process. Online I have also found that laptop enthusiast frequently demonstrate through "thermal pad" modifications that connecting the processor's cooling system directly to the aluminum chassis can reduce processor temperatures and improve sustained performance during prolonged workloads by allowing the entire enclosure to participate in heat dissipation. Numerous online thermal modification tests report sustained performance improvements of approximately 10–25% during processor-intensive workloads after increasing chassis participation in heat spreading.

Building on this concept, I propose redesigning the laptop chassis as a multifunctional thermal architecture rather than simply a structural enclosure. Instead of treating the aluminum chassis as passive structure, it becomes an engineered heat spreader that distributes processor heat across a much larger surface area. Increasing the effective heat rejection area lowers heat flux and reduces localized hot spots, allowing processor temperatures to remain lower during extended workloads. Rather than uniformly heating every external surface, high-conductivity pathways within the chassis would preferentially direct heat toward regions that users rarely touch, such as the rear hinge and side edges. High-contact regions including the palm rests and bottom center would remain thermally isolated through selective insulation or low-conductivity interface materials.

This concept offers several advantages beyond simply lowering processor temperatures. Lower operating temperatures reduce thermal throttling, allowing the processor to maintain higher clock speeds for longer periods while completing computational tasks more quickly. Faster task completion allows the processor to return to low-power idle states sooner, reducing the total energy required to complete a given workload. Additionally, improved passive heat spreading reduces reliance on active cooling fans, lowering both electrical power consumption and acoustic noise. Finally, integrating thermal management into an existing structural component aligns with modern product design trends emphasizing multifunctional engineering, where a single component performs several functions simultaneously instead of adding additional parts.

The primary challenges involve balancing thermal performance with user comfort while maintaining a lightweight, manufacturable product. Although spreading heat across the chassis lowers localized temperatures, users are sensitive to warm surfaces, particularly on the palm rests and bottom panel. Additional thermal pathways must therefore be carefully engineered to maximize heat rejection while maintaining acceptable touch temperatures. Manufacturing complexity, material selection, battery isolation, and long-term durability must also be considered before such a design could become commercially viable.

Overall, this progression—from generating energy, to recovering waste energy, to reducing energy waste altogether—reflects how my engineering thinking has evolved. Rather than asking how to produce more electricity, I ultimately concluded that the greatest opportunity lies in redesigning the laptop itself so it simply requires less energy to perform the same task. I believe this multifunctional thermal chassis provides the strongest balance between engineering feasibility, user experience, and meaningful improvements in laptop energy efficiency.

Question 4 — Selective Thermal Architecture

Question 4

Deep dive into one design. This means describe in more detail how the new design would be built and tested to verify the new performance target. Clearly show your work!

My Response

The objective of this redesign is to improve the energy efficiency of a laptop during everyday web browsing by reducing processor operating temperature without increasing system complexity or requiring additional active cooling. Rather than adding larger fans or additional cooling hardware, this design reimagines the laptop chassis as a multifunctional structural and thermal component. I believe efficiency comes from using every component of a device to its fullest. Rather than treating the aluminum chassis as only a structural enclosure, I questioned whether it could also become a thermal management system that routes heat toward areas users rarely touch while maintaining the thin form factor expected of modern laptops.

Current cooling in the Microsoft Surface Laptop Studio2 is highly localized. Heat generated by the CPU and GPU is transferred through a thermal interface material into copper heat pipes, which transport the heat to compact fin stacks where dual blower fans force air through the fins and reject the heat into the surrounding environment. Although premium laptops such as Apple's fanless MacBook Air already use graphite sheets and the aluminum chassis to assist with passive heat spreading, the enclosure primarily functions as a thermal reservoir. User comfort is prioritized over maximizing passive heat rejection, leaving much of the chassis thermally underutilized

Instead of redesigning the cooling system itself, I chose to redesign the chassis. My proposed design transforms the existing aluminum enclosure into a selective thermal architecture. The existing active cooling mechanisms remain unchanged and continue transporting heat away from the CPU. Rather than terminating at the conventional heat sink or fan, however, heat now also dissipates into a laser-patterned pyrolytic graphite sheet.

Pyrolytic graphite was selected because it already exists in modern consumer electronics and possesses an exceptionally high in-plane thermal conductivity, typically between 1000 and 1700 W/m·K, while remaining extremely thin and lightweight. Currently, Apple uses graphite as one large rectangular sheet that spreads heat relatively uniformly throughout the chassis. Rather than inventing a new material, I propose laser cutting the graphite into a branching thermal network that functions like a printed circuit board, routing heat instead of electricity.

Wide graphite branches with very low thermal resistance connect the processor to designated radiator regions located along the rear structural beam immediately in front of the display hinge, and the left and right side rails. These are regions that are naturally exposed to ambient air but rarely experience prolonged contact with the user's skin. High-contact regions, including the palm rests and bottom chassis, intentionally receive no direct graphite pathways. Instead, any heat reaching these areas must travel only through the aluminum chassis itself, which has a significantly lower thermal conductivity than the graphite network. Rather than actively moving heat toward specific regions, the design simply creates a path of much lower thermal resistance, allowing the majority of heat to naturally flow toward desired locations.

In order to prevent heat directed to radiator regions from traveling through the chassis to high user-contact areas, I first thought of dividing the chassis into multiple pieces separated by insulating materials. While this would reduce heat transfer, it conflicts with the seamless unibody construction used by premium laptops and would increase manufacturing complexity while reducing perceived product quality.

Instead, I looked toward thermal engineering. Since heat naturally follows the path of least thermal resistance, I suggest manipulating the geometry of the chassis itself. Rather than separating the laptop into multiple components, I propose machining internal thermal necks between the designated ‘hot’ and ‘cool’ regions. These thermal necks are created by locally reducing the internal cross-sectional area of the aluminum while leaving the exterior appearance completely unchanged. According to Fourier's Law of heat conduction, reducing the conduction area increases thermal resistance, slowing the movement of heat between regions without introducing additional materials or visible seams. From the outside, the laptop still appears as one continuous aluminum unibody, but internally the chassis functions as a thermally engineered network.

To reject the heat once it reached the designated radiator regions, my first instinct was to simply add ventilation holes. However, I found that manufacturers generally avoid unnecessary openings because they create pathways for dust, moisture, and debris to enter the computer. Rather than exposing the internals, I shifted toward increasing the effective surface area of the radiator regions themselves.

The underside of the rear structural beam and side rails would incorporate shallow CNC-machined microchannels approximately 0.3–0.5 mm deep. Unlike traditional heat sink fins, these microchannels remain hidden beneath the laptop, preserving the clean design while significantly increasing surface area available for natural convection. The rear structural beam would also be raised approximately 0.5–1.0 mm above the surrounding chassis, creating a passive chimney that enhances natural convection behind the display without additional fans or ventilation openings.

To further improve passive heat rejection, radiator regions would receive a high-emissivity hard-anodized coating. Polished aluminum has an emissivity of approximately 0.05, radiating heat relatively poorly. Hard-anodized aluminum, however, exhibits emissivities approaching 0.85–0.90, allowing the same component to reject considerably more heat through thermal radiation while maintaining corrosion resistance and durability.

This design contains no completely new cooling technology. Instead, existing technologies are reorganized into a different thermal architecture. The primary heat transport layer remains. The laser-patterned graphite sheet becomes the thermal routing layer, intentionally directing some heat toward preferred regions. Finally, the aluminum chassis becomes the distributed heat rejection layer, rejecting heat through strategically engineered radiator zones rather than treating the entire enclosure equally.

The concept could first be validated without building a completely new device by modifying an existing laptop. A replacement bottom chassis could be CNC machined with the proposed thermal necks, rear microchannels, and structural beam geometry while leaving the motherboard, processor, and existing cooling hardware unchanged. Commercial pyrolytic graphite sheets could then be laser cut according to the proposed thermal routing network and bonded to the existing copper heat pipe assembly using commercially available thermally conductive adhesive. This isolates the chassis and parallel heat routing as the only modified variables.

ANSYS Icepak simulations would compare heat flow, processor temperature, and chassis temperatures between the stock and redesigned devices under identical web browsing workloads.Following simulation, the physical prototype would be evaluated experimentally using identical workloads while logging CPU package power, processor temperature, clock frequency, and fan speed through HWiNFO64. A thermal camera can visualize how heat spreads across the chassis, verifying whether the graphite network successfully redirects heat toward the rear structural beam and side rails while maintaining comfortable temperatures beneath the user's hands.

The primary performance target is to reduce average processor operating temperature by approximately 5–10°C during sustained web browsing while maintaining palm rest temperatures below approximately 35°C and bottom chassis temperatures below approximately 40°C. The rear structural beam and side rails may safely operate at higher temperatures because they experience minimal user interaction. If successful, the processor should experience less thermal throttling, reduced fan usage, and complete computational tasks more quickly before returning to low-power idle states. Rather than simply making the laptop cooler, this design fundamentally changes how heat moves throughout the device. The chassis transitions from a passive structural enclosure into a multifunctional thermal management system that simultaneously improves cooling performance, energy efficiency, and user experience.

Question 5 — Design Summary & Key Learning

Question 5

Summarize your work. In a single page, create a simple, clear and complete summary. Include 1 new key learning from completing this challenge.

My Response

This project challenged me to rethink how a product I use every day could become more energy efficient through mechanical design. Revisiting the laptop allowed me to build upon research I began in high school using the mechanical engineering knowledge I have gained over the past two years.

I began by analyzing the complete power pathway of my Microsoft Surface Laptop Studio 2 during a typical web browsing session. Using battery discharge measurements, Windows battery reports, and first-principles energy calculations, I established a quantitative baseline for the laptop's average power consumption before even considering how to improve it.

My initial thought was that I needed to invent something completely new.I spent hours digging through research papers on everything from piezoelectric and triboelectric energy harvesting to spacecraft thermal management, Formula 1 cooling systems, refrigeration, and battery technology. I kept asking myself, what cutting-edge technology could I borrow and adapt into a laptop?

From there, I explored several redesign concepts, beginning by revisiting my high school research on piezoelectric computer keys before investigating thermoelectric energy recovery. While both concepts demonstrated that energy could theoretically be harvested from the user or from waste heat, the amount of recoverable energy was simply too small to meaningfully improve the efficiency of a laptop. Every new idea added complexity while recovering very little power.

That realization completely changed my approach. Instead of asking how I could generate more energy, I started asking how I could stop wasting so much of it in the first place.

It took a surprisingly long time before I finally stepped back and looked at what was already there. I didn't want to completely reinvent the laptop. Instead, I wanted to understand the technologies that already exist and see if they could simply be organized more effectively. While researching thermal management, I found countless Reddit discussions and YouTube videos of enthusiasts modifying their own laptops. One modification kept appearing over and over again: adding thermal pads that transfer processor heat into the aluminum chassis. Users consistently reported lower processor temperatures and better sustained performance because the chassis acted as a larger heat spreader, but the laptop also became noticeably hotter to touch.

That observation became the turning point of the project. Instead of thinking purely as an engineer, I started thinking like a customer. I realized I almost always touch the palm rests and bottom panel, but rarely the rear hinge or side rails. If those regions are already exposed aluminum and are rarely touched, why shouldn't they become the primary locations for passive heat rejection? Instead of treating the entire chassis equally, the laptop could intelligently decide where heat should be released.

This led to my final concept: Selective Thermal Architecture. Rather than replacing the existing cooling system, the design introduces a parallel passive cooling pathway using laser-patterned pyrolytic graphite sheets to route heat toward designated radiator regions along the rear structural beam and side rails. Internal thermal necks increase thermal resistance between these radiator regions and high-contact areas such as the palm rests, while CNC-machined microchannels and high-emissivity coatings improve passive heat rejection without compromising the clean unibody design of the laptop.

One aspect of this concept that I appreciate most is its practicality. Rather than relying on emerging technologies that I do not yet fully understand, such as advanced phase-change cooling systems or entirely new materials, I built the design around technologies that already exist in modern laptops and simply reorganized them into a different thermal architecture. Because the proposal functions as a parallel passive cooling system, I believe it could be adapted to many existing laptops without requiring an entirely new computer architecture.

The most valuable lesson I learned from this challenge is that good engineering is not always about inventing completely new technology, but about understanding existing systems well enough to reorganize them into something more effective. I began this project believing the answer to sustainability was generating additional clean energy. I finished it realizing that the greatest opportunity often lies in designing products that simply require less energy to accomplish the same task. That shift in perspective fundamentally changed how I think about engineering design, and it will influence the products I design moving forward.
