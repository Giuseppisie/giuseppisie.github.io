Challenge Overview

Technical design application · Five-question response and supporting documentation

This page documents my response to Apple's Technical Design Challenge, beginning with an everyday powered object and tracing the engineering systems that enable its use. I chose my laptop, focusing on web browsing and research as a daily use case.

The investigation builds on my earlier work studying piezoelectric energy harvesting in computer keyboards, connecting an interest in sustainable electronics with mechanical engineering and user-centered design.

Challenge Questions

Question 1 — Everyday Object & Power Path

Question 1

Describe why you chose the object in your house used every day that requires power. Describe the main use case for the object. How you interact with the object. Describe the power path for this object to enable your daily use case.

My Response

I have been interested in the intersection of user-facing product design and clean energy since sophomore year of high school, when I spent two years researching energy harvesting technologies in consumer electronics. My project focused on designing a piezoelectric computer key capable of converting typing forces into electricity. Although the prototype demonstrated that a potential difference can be generated by combining piezoelectric ceramics and into a computer key, I also became aware that this is not the most efficient solution due to the incredibly low current of the ceramics. When I saw this assignment, I knew it was an opportunity to revisit those research questions while applying the mechanical engineering knowledge I have gained over the past two years to rethink how computers could become more energy efficient.

I use a computer every day, as do billions of people around the world. Whether I am searching for a new recipe, sending emails, completing coursework, or reading research papers, my computer serves as my primary connection to information. While industries such as transportation, lighting, and household appliances have made highly visible advances toward sustainability, consumer electronics have seen comparatively little attention despite being used constantly across the globe. This made the computer an ideal object for my design exploration.

The primary use case I will analyze is web browsing and online research. During a typical session, I repeatedly type search queries, read articles, and continue refining my searches. My interaction with the computer begins with the keyboard, where each keystroke sends an electrical signal to the processor. A Wi-Fi connection transmits my request across the internet, while the processor performs millions of calculations to retrieve and organize the requested information. Finally, the graphics processor and display controller generate the image that appears on the screen, allowing me to read the results before repeating the process again.

The power path that enables this seemingly simple interaction is surprisingly complex. Electrical energy enters the computer from a wall outlet as alternating current (AC). Inside the charger, it is converted into direct current (DC) before charging the battery and supplying the system. The battery management system and power management integrated circuit regulate and distribute electricity to the processor, graphics processor, memory, storage drive, wireless networking hardware, display, keyboard, and other supporting electronics. Each component performs a specific function, but nearly all of the electrical energy entering the computer ultimately becomes heat. As I continue typing and searching, I can often feel the device warming beneath my hands; a reminder that the processor is working continuously to execute billions of operations. Understanding this complete energy pathway provides the foundation for investigating how mechanical design can reduce energy consumption rather than simply managing the heat produced afterward.
