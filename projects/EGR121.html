EGR 121 — Engineering Design & Communication

Course Overview

EGR 121 introduces first years to the process of team-based creative conceptualization, visualization, prototyping, and product realization. Students use computer-aided design tools to create custom circuit boards and CNC-machined components to produce prototype systems.

Design concepts are introduced and reinforced through hands-on assignments emphasizing collaboration, documentation, and iterative improvement.

DC2 — Mousetrap Car

A mechanical energy conversion challenge focused on translating the stored energy of a single mousetrap into controlled motion across a sandy surface. Our team iterated on wheel geometry, drivetrain ratios, and traction strategies to optimize performance under strict constraints.

DC3 — Happy Meal Toy

A consumer product design project emphasizing manufacturability, safety, and cost. Our team designed a modular toy system with full CAD models, tolerancing, and manufacturing documentation intended for mass production.

DC4 — Rube Goldberg Machine

A large-scale collaborative Rube Goldberg machine integrating mechanical and electronic subsystems. Our four-person subteam built themed modules inspired by Legally Blonde and Titanic, each using custom circuitry to trigger sequential events.

What my team designed and why

Below is a concise, bulleted summary of our DC2 design choices (Full memo attached):

  • Triangular chassis: Chosen because angling the mousetrap lever increased the length of string travel in the final ~30° of rotation, giving more consistent energy transfer; the triangular shape also allowed weight reduction by removing material from faces while maintaining stiffness.
  • Cardboard (large, smooth) driving wheels: Cardboard wheels provided a good balance of low weight, sufficient diameter to avoid sinking in sand, and a moderate moment of inertia—smooth, larger wheels outperformed heavily treaded options under the available torque.
  • Lever & dowel choice: A wooden dowel lever (tested 4" vs 8") was selected for its combination of stiffness, low mass, and ease of attachment; lever length was finalized after prototyping to balance torque and string travel.
  • Bearings for guiding wheels: Bearings used on non-driving wheels reduced axle friction and helped the car roll further in sand; driving wheels mounted directly produced a simpler drive interface to the axle.
  • Adding weight (rear washers): After initial lever tuning, metal washers were added at the rear to improve traction and balance without sinking the vehicle; mass placement was tuned for best distance vs carrying capacity.
  • Release method & robustness: Because the vehicle must be released by cutting a string in a 6-minute testing window, we prioritized durability and repeatable setup.

These decisions were arrived at via iterative prototyping and a Pugh-matrix approach detailed in the team memo. Click the button below to open the full memo (PDF).

What my team designed and why

Summary of our DC3 'Happy Zoo Train' toy design choices and rationale (full memo attached):

  • Mix-and-match peg-hole system: We designed a consistent peg & hole interface so every car top fits any frame; this encourages collecting and replay value by letting kids connect cars into longer trains.
  • Generic, paintable animal bodies: The body car uses a single injection-molded shape that can be painted as different animals (pig, tiger, etc.), lowering tooling cost while increasing collectible variety.
  • Injection-moldable geometry: Parts were designed with moldability in mind (no overhangs, separate conductor figure) to allow efficient mass production and minimize post-processing.
  • Tolerances for snap-fit & durability: We used ~0.15 mm CTF tolerances on critical features (peg holes, conductor legs) so parts snap together securely but remain separable for play.
  • No choking hazards & safety: Each toy was sized to avoid small removable hazards; wheel pins are glued after assembly to ensure permanence where needed.
  • Material & sustainability choices: Polyethylene (PE) was selected—lightweight, durable, recyclable—and the car tops are shelled to reduce material use and cost.
  • Manufacturing cost awareness: The design prioritized few, low-cost parts (frame, tops, wheels, pins, conductor) and simple painting/stenciling for branding to keep per-unit cost low (detailed cost breakdown in memo).

These points and the manufacturing/assembly decisions (wheel pins glued, shelling to reduce plastic, cost estimates) are documented in our DC3 Technical Memo (see button below). For detailed drawings, tolerances, and the production plan, open the memo.
